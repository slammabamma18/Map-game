# Cradle of Nations

## Little Worlds (`sandbox.html`)

A second game in the WorldBox mould, built from scratch: a small painted pixel world you can terraform with brushes (land, sea, mountain, sand, forest), creatures you drop by hand (humans, elves, orcs, dwarves, sheep, wolves) that hunt, forage, fish, cut trees, carry wood and build houses tile by tile, found villages with named kingdoms, borders and flags, crown kings, raise soldiers and go to war, and a toolbar of physical powers (lightning, spreading fire, meteors with craters, bombs, plague, curse, tornado, rain, blessing, healing, fertility) plus world laws. Everything is visible at once and every power acts on tiles and creatures directly. It is a single file with no dependencies.

A single-file world-history simulation: drop settlers on a real map and watch villages, nations, trade and wars unfold from the Stone Age to the Information Age. Everything is in `index.html` (open it in any browser; it is also published as a claude.ai artifact).

## Art pipeline

The simulation is untouched; the whole renderer (the second half of the script) was rebuilt around a few ideas that make the map look hand-painted while staying cheap on phones:

- **Terrain relief map.** The world is painted once into a 2x-resolution canvas: biome colours with noise mottling, hill-shading from a smoothed height field, domain-warped biome edges so cells do not read as squares, rounded coastlines with shallow water and foam, rivers as soft strokes, snow caps and dune ripples. Soil blessings, blight and fallout go into a separate cheap tint layer so a nuke never repaints the world.
- **People.** Civilians are Kenney's mini characters baked as eight-frame walk cycles with idle, gathering, carrying, spear-thrust and sitting frames; soldiers are KayKit's adventurers (barbarians in the first ages, knights and crossbowmen in the middle ones) with one weapon each, baked walking, standing, attacking and blocking. Crowds scale with population, from a handful in a camp to well over a hundred strolling the streets of a metropolis, with a per-frame budget that the quality governor trims on slow devices.
- **Rendered assets.** Buildings, people, animals, trees, vehicles and boats are real 3D models, all CC0: KayKit's Medieval Hexagon Pack and City Builder Bits (Kay Lousberg), Kenney's City Kit, Nature Kit, Car Kit, Mini Characters, pirate ships and city-builder kit, and Quaternius's farm animals and wildlife. `tools/render` (a three.js page driven by Playwright) renders every model under one oblique orthographic camera and one key light with soft shadows at 200 px per map cell, packs the crops into `sheet.png` with `atlas.json`, and the build embeds both in `index.html`. Characters are baked as walk-cycle, idle, carry and work frames from the models' own animations. Fields, water, smoke and fire remain procedural.
- **Sprite atlas.** Every sheet crop and remaining procedural sprite is cut once into a small canvas at the current zoom and stamped with `drawImage`. Sprites are regenerated at the exact zoom once a pinch settles so every stamp is an unscaled, pixel-aligned copy.
- **Settlement plans.** Towns are no longer laid out on the tile grid. Each settlement gets a seeded plan: radial streets with a ring road for organic eras, rotated grids for classical and modern ones, buildings placed along both sides of the streets facing them, then infilled; civic buildings (market, tavern, blacksmith, church, well, barracks) are sprinkled through, the capital's landmark stands at the centre (castle, palace, tower), walled eras get a stone ring with towers and a gate, and construction shows as timber-frame stages.
- **View caches.** The composed terrain layers and the static scenery (fields, trees, roads, town ground, buildings, harbours, mine works) are rasterised into canvases that cover the view plus a margin. Panning only copies them, pinching scales them, and they are repainted when the view leaves the margin or the zoom settles. Only animated details (walkers, animals, birds, smoke, windmill blades, boats, traffic, construction, water glints) are drawn every frame.
- **Field parcels.** Worked land is no longer one square per tile. Each settlement seeds a jittered lattice (garden plots, then long strips, then medieval patchwork, then fenced blocks and modern pivot fields) whose Voronoi cells are clipped to the land, filled with the plot's crop and season colour, furrowed along their own axis and edged with earth banks, hedges, stone walls or fences.
- **Flowing land.** Biome colours are sampled from a smoothed, domain-warped colour field so forest, grass and snow edges meander instead of stepping along cells; mountains carry ridge glyphs at region zoom, rivers widen into the sea, the political overlay is built at double resolution through the same warp, and a mip of the terrain serves the world view.
- **Roads that follow the land.** Each road is a short A* over the cells between two towns that avoids water, forest, steep ground and river crossings, then smoothed; traffic (porters, wagons, cars, trains) runs along the same path and roads widen with the towns they join.
- **Day and night.** A five-minute day tints the map warm at dawn and dusk and blue at night; windows light up, street lamps glow along modern streets, cloud shadows drift across the land, waves break on the shore and distance haze softens the world view.
- **Each age marks the land.** Stone-age and early villages sit in clearings of stumps behind a palisade, industrial towns raise smokestacks, modern ones pave a square, plant a park and light their streets; market stalls fill medieval squares, fishermen stand on the piers and flocks wheel over the woods.
- **Adaptive quality.** Frame time is tracked and slow devices drop the water shimmer first, then crowd sizes.

## Peopling the world

Settlements no longer fall into a lattice: each site blocks an irregular radius around it, bands prefer rivers, coasts and rich soil, and a slow regional noise makes some country more attractive than other, so towns cluster along water and leave poorer land empty.

One band of settlers is enough to fill the planet. Stone Age camps split often; bands that find the land around them taken strike out far, ranging up to a hundred cells over land and across straits and shallow seas, preferring empty country and new regions. A band that arrives on poor ground keeps walking, and a camp that cannot feed itself moves on as nomads. Each far settlement takes the culture of the land it finds, so new peoples appear as the migrations spread. Early eras last longer, so continents fill with hunter-gatherers and farmers long before the first empires: from a seed in East Africa, bands reach Europe and Asia in the Bronze Age; from China, the Americas are settled over the Bering land bridge by the Classical era.

## Your people

The first settlers you place become your people. Every camp, town and nation their descendants found carries their line, wherever the migrations take them, so the lineage can be followed from one camp in East Africa to nations on five continents. After the first camp you choose an ambition for them (fill the world, ten million souls, an empire of sixty towns, a city of a million, a faith followed in sixty towns, the Information Age within six thousand years, fifty distinct peoples); the world tries to fulfil it on its own and the saga sheet tracks progress, milestones (new continents, new ages, first nation, first city, nations that fall) and the favour your people earn as they prosper. Favour pays for the god powers: blessings, enlightenment, plagues, meteors, wars and bombs now cost it, and three new powers spend it on slow influence instead of instant miracles: an Exodus sends a band from a town to distant land, a Prophet founds a faith among your people, and a Golden Age lifts a nation's learning and wealth for three centuries. If the last of your people dies, their story ends; the list of past worlds in the saga sheet remembers every people you have seeded.

## The first ten minutes

The welcome card is three sentences and a Begin button, with the full guide folded underneath. Placing settlers drops the camera onto the camp at 3× speed, where years pass at a pace that lets huts rise and bands set out while you watch; the first two ages are shorter than before and 1× runs five ticks a second. A short guided arc surfaces one hint at a time as the people reach each step: speeding time, following a band, spending favour, the first nation, zooming out to see the spread. The people bar sits under the stats line and toasts stack below it.

## The crew

Every settlement's people have real jobs, and the camera can watch any of them. Choppers walk to a standing tree, swing an axe until it falls (it stays a stump and regrows over a few minutes), pick up the log and carry it to the building site; a builder works the pile and the hut rises through a ring of posts, wattle walls and finally a thatched roof. Fishers cast from the shore or riverbank with a rod and float, land the fish and hang it on the drying rack. Hunters stalk game that grazes on the foraging grounds and flees when spooked, throw a spear, and carry the kill to the hearth; gatherers fill baskets at the bushes. The simulation keeps a site ledger per settlement (worker-years and timber paid in) that decides when a building completes; the crew makes that progress visible, and the site has its own slot in the plan so huts accumulate as you watch. Stone Age camps use procedurally modelled thatched huts, hide tents, a hearth, a woodpile, a fish rack and a totem; Neolithic villages add longhouses and raised granaries. Time runs at one year a second at 1×.

## Watching the people

When the first settlers are placed the camera drops to their camp, where hunters walk out to the foraging grounds with spears and come back with game, gatherers fill baskets, and builders raise huts one at a time. Construction is paid in hands: every settlement accumulates labour from its people, and a building is finished only when enough worker-years and timber have gone into it, so a camp of ten raises a hut every twenty years or so while a city keeps three sites busy. Settler bands and wanderers are drawn as files of walkers with their loads and animals, or as boats when the way is water, with a gold mark over your own people; armies are files of soldiers with shields and a banner on the march, a block when they stand, and spearmen at work under siege. The saga sheet lists your travellers with a Watch button that follows them until the map is dragged.

## Rebuilding the sprite sheet

The render tooling lives in the session scratch directory during development (`render/index.html`, `bake.js`, the packer) and needs the model packs cloned next to it; see the commit message for sources. Rendering all 300 sprites takes about half a minute in headless Chromium with SwiftShader.

Debug hooks on `window.__cradle`: `spriteSheet(zoom)` lays out every sprite, `toggle(pass, bool)` switches render passes, `PROF` and `QUAL` expose timings and the quality level.
